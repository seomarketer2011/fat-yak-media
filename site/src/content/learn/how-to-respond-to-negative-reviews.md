Every business gets one eventually. What separates the professionals from the flame wars is the reply. Here's the playbook.

## First, wait an hour — then don't wait a week

Reply angry and you'll lose the reader; the internet is littered with businesses that torched their reputation in a comment. But silence has a cost too — a fresh complaint sitting unanswered looks like guilt. The sweet spot: same day or next, once you're calm and have checked the facts.

## Write for the audience, not the author

This is the mental shift that fixes everything. The angry customer may never come back — fine. The reply is really for the hundreds of future customers who will read this exchange while deciding whether to call you. They're not judging whether you were right. They're judging what you'd be like to deal with when something goes wrong.

## The four-line formula

**Thank, acknowledge, act, move offline.** *"Thanks for the feedback, and I'm sorry the install ran late. We've changed how we schedule Fridays so this doesn't happen again. Please give me a call directly — I'd like to make it right."* Specific, unpanicked, human. No lawyering, no "as per our terms", no essay.

## When the review is unfair or fake

State your position once, briefly and courteously — "we have no record of a customer by this name" — then flag it via your dashboard and let it go. Never speculate publicly about who it "really" is. Readers are good at spotting unhinged reviews on their own; your composed reply is what they'll remember.

## The quiet upside

A well-handled negative review is social proof money can't buy. It's also why chasing a spotless 5.0 is the wrong goal — a 4.8 with visible, graceful recoveries is more believable and more persuasive. The real defence, though, is volume: a [steady stream of good reviews](/learn/how-to-get-more-google-reviews/) makes any single bad one a rounding error.
