Reviews are word of mouth with a permanent record. They're also one of the strongest levers for [showing up on Google Maps](/learn/why-your-business-doesnt-show-up-on-google-maps/). Here's the routine that fills them up.

## Ask at the peak, not the invoice

The best moment to ask is when the customer is happiest: the job's just done, the problem's solved, they've just said thank you. Asking then feels natural and lands almost every time. Asking a week later, attached to an invoice, feels like admin and gets ignored.

## Make it one tap

Nobody will "go to Google and search for us and click reviews". Get your direct review link from your Business Profile dashboard and put it everywhere: a text after the job, a QR code on the counter or the back of the van, a line in your email signature. The gap between "willing" and "done" is friction — remove it.

## Give the words a starting push

Many happy customers stall at the blank text box. A gentle prompt helps: "If you mention the suburb and what we did, it helps other locals find us." That's not scripting the review — it's curing blank-page syndrome, and those details genuinely help your visibility.

## Reply to every single one

A short, human reply to every review — grateful for the good, calm and constructive for the bad — does double duty. Google sees an active business; future customers see someone who gives a damn. (Bad ones deserve their own playbook: [how to respond to negative reviews](/learn/how-to-respond-to-negative-reviews/).)

## Never buy, trade or filter

Purchased reviews, review swaps, and asking only the customers you're sure about are all against Google's rules, and profiles do get suspended for it. Beyond the risk: readers can smell it. Ten real reviews mentioning real jobs beat fifty generic five-star one-liners.

## Make it a system, not a memory

The difference between businesses with 12 reviews and businesses with 200 isn't charm — it's routine. The ask happens every time, automatically, because it's built into how jobs close.
