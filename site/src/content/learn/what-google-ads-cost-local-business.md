"What does it cost" deserves actual numbers, not "it depends" — so here are the numbers, and the arithmetic that makes them mean something.

## What a click costs

Every time someone searches, Google auctions the ad spots. Your price per click depends on how many competitors want that same search. Local rule of thumb: low-competition services see clicks for a few dollars; busy trades commonly pay $5–$15; urgent, high-value searches — emergency plumber, locksmith at midnight, injury lawyer — can run $20–$50 a click, because one job repays a lot of clicks.

## What a customer costs — the number that matters

Say clicks cost $6 and one visitor in ten calls you: a phone call costs $60. If you close half of those calls, a customer costs $120. Whether ads "work" is now plain arithmetic: worth more than $120, scale up; worth less, fix the leaks or don't run ads. Every ad conversation should end at this number — which is why we [track every call to its ad](/services/ppc/) before spending a cent.

## What a sensible budget looks like

At $30–$50 a day, most local businesses buy enough clicks to generate steady enquiries and — just as important — enough data to judge honestly. A month at that rate is a real test. Two hundred dollars "to try it out" isn't; it buys too few clicks to prove anything either way.

## Where budgets actually leak

The classic failure isn't overpaying per click — it's paying for the wrong clicks entirely. Searches from suburbs you don't serve. Job-seekers googling your trade. DIYers hunting instructions. Without weekly pruning and a list of searches to exclude, a third of spend can quietly vanish into clicks that could never become customers. This, more than bid tricks, is what professional management earns its fee fixing.

## When ads are the right call

Ads shine when you need volume *now* — a new business, a quiet season, spare capacity to fill — and as the bridge while [free rankings are being earned](/learn/how-long-does-it-take-to-show-up-on-google/).
